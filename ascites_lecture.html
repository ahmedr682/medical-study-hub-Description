Section 1

Definition, causes and differential diagnosis

Definition

Ascites is abnormal accumulation of fluid in the abdomen. The lecture states that it is more than 25 mL of fluid in the peritoneal cavity. It also gives the Greek/Latin historical meaning as “bag-like dropsy”.

Causes

General causes (as part of generalized anasarca):

  • Hepatic: liver cirrhosis and hepatic venous outflow obstruction.
  • Cardiac: congestive heart failure and constrictive pericarditis.
  • Renal: nephrotic syndrome.
  • Nutritional: hypoalbuminemia.

Local causes: malignant ascites, tuberculous ascites, pancreatic ascites, bowel perforation and Meigs syndrome.

Section 2

SAAG classification and pathophysiology

SAAG

SAAG = serum albumin − ascitic fluid albumin.

CategorySAAGCauses in the lecture
Portal hypertensive ascites≥1.1 g/dLHepatic and cardiac.
Non-portal hypertensive ascites<1.1 g/dLRenal, malignant and inflammatory ascites.

Transudate vs exudate (lecture description)

  • Transudate: related to increased portal venous pressure (>8 mmHg, usually around 20 mmHg in the lecture, e.g. cirrhosis); low protein (<3 g/dL), low LDH, high pH, normal glucose and fewer than 1 WBC per 1000 mm³.
  • Exudate: actively secreted fluid due to inflammation or malignancy; high protein and LDH, low pH (<7.30), low glucose and more WBCs.
  • The lecture states that SAAG <1.1 g/dL implies an exudate.

Mechanism of cirrhotic ascites

  1. Portal hypertension raises capillary hydrostatic pressure in the splanchnic bed.
  2. Fluid becomes sequestered in the abdomen.
  3. Reduced renal perfusion stimulates blood-pressure regulatory hormones, notably aldosterone.
  4. Sympathetic nervous system activity and renin production increase.
  5. Renal tubular sodium reabsorption rises, promoting further fluid retention.
Section 3

Features and examination of cirrhotic ascites

Symptoms

  • Weight gain.
  • Increasing abdominal distension.
  • Dyspnea.

Examination

Look for stigmata of cirrhosis: muscle wasting, palmar erythema and spider naevi.

Inspection:

  • Signs of chronic increased abdominal pressure: obtuse subcostal angle, divarication of recti, downward shifting of the umbilicus and abdominal hernias.
  • Feminine hair distribution.
  • Dilated abdominal veins.

Percussion: shifting dullness or transmitted thrill may detect ascites; pleural effusion may coexist.

Auscultation: a rare venous hum (machinery murmur) between the xiphisternum and umbilicus, due to recanalization of the paraumbilical vein.

Peripheral edema usually follows ascites, according to the lecture.

Section 4

Investigations and diagnosis

Ultrasonography

  • Can quantify the amount of ascites.
  • Provides good images of the liver, portal vein and spleen.

CT scan

  • Adds value in detecting abdominal masses.

Ascitic fluid study — diagnostic paracentesis

Physical appearance: under normal conditions fluid is clear or pale yellow; cirrhotic ascites is clear or straw-coloured.

Chemical and cytological description of cirrhotic ascites in the lecture: transudate, low protein (<3 g/dL), low LDH, high pH, normal glucose and fewer than 1 WBC per 1000 mm³.

Other modalities shown

  • Ultrasonography.
  • Diagnostic paracentesis.
  • Laparoscopy.
Section 5

Complications and refractory ascites

  1. Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP): diagnosed in the lecture with ascitic fluid neutrophil count >250/mm³. Abdominal pain and fever are common symptoms. The lecture lists cefotaxime 2 g every 8 hours for 5 days as treatment.
  2. Hepatic hydrothorax: commonly right-sided effusion.
  3. Abdominal hernias: commonly umbilical.
  4. Refractory ascites.
  5. Hepatorenal syndrome (HRS).

Refractory ascites definitions in the lecture

  • Diuretic-resistant ascites: failure of mobilization despite diet regimen and maximum diuretic doses (spironolactone 400 mg and furosemide 160 mg).
  • Intractable ascites: inability to reach full diuretic doses because of side effects such as encephalopathy or electrolyte imbalance.

Causes listed: non-compliance, infection (e.g. SBP or TB on top of cirrhosis) and malignancy on top of liver cirrhosis.

Section 6

Treatment according to SAAG and refractory status

High-SAAG (transudative) ascites in cirrhosis

Diet: salt restriction is the initial treatment (“no added salt” strategy). Water restriction is needed if serum sodium falls below 130 mmol/L.

Diuretics

  • Because salt restriction is the basic concept and aldosterone increases salt retention, the lecture seeks a medication that counteracts aldosterone.
  • Spironolactone is the drug of choice because it blocks aldosterone receptors in the collecting tubule.
  • Starting dose described: oral spironolactone 100 mg/day; maximum 400 mg/day.
  • The lecture states 40% respond to spironolactone.
  • For nonresponders, add loop diuretic: furosemide 40 mg/day; maximum 160 mg/day.
  • Diuretics are described as once daily.
  • Monitor diuresis by daily weight. Goal: weight loss no more than 1.0 kg/day with ascites plus peripheral edema, and no more than 0.5 kg/day with ascites alone.

Paracentesis and surgery

  • Severe (tense) or refractory ascites may need therapeutic paracentesis.
  • Salt-free albumin is generally given IV in proportion to the amount of ascites removed.
  • Ascites refractory to medical therapy (spironolactone 400 mg/day and furosemide 160 mg/day) is considered an indication for liver transplantation.
  • Shunts mentioned for selected advanced cases: portacaval, peritoneovenous and TIPS. The lecture says these have not been shown to extend life expectancy and are considered bridges to transplantation.

Refractory ascites

Treatment options listed: paracentesis with or without salt-free albumin, TIPS and liver transplantation (stated as treatment of choice).

Low-SAAG (“exudative”) ascites

Generally does not respond to salt-balance manipulation or diuretic therapy. Repeated paracentesis and treatment of the underlying cause (infection or malignancy) are the mainstays described.

Safety note: Drug doses and SBP treatment above are transcribed as lecture content for study. They are not individualized prescribing instructions; follow current hospital protocols and senior guidance.

Clinical Cases

Practice connecting lecture facts to a clinical presentation.

Case 1

Case — New ascites in cirrhosis

A patient with known cirrhosis develops progressive abdominal distension and weight gain.

Clinical reasoning

Ascites is likely. The lecture lists ultrasound to quantify fluid and assess liver/portal vein/spleen, and diagnostic paracentesis for fluid analysis. Calculate SAAG to classify the ascites.

Case 2

Case — Fever and abdominal pain

A patient with ascites develops fever and abdominal pain.

Clinical reasoning

Consider SBP. The lecture gives ascitic neutrophils >250/mm³ as the diagnostic threshold and lists cefotaxime 2 g every 8 hours for 5 days; actual treatment must follow current protocols and senior assessment.

Case 3

Case — Persistent fluid despite diuretics

A patient with cirrhosis has persistent ascites despite dietary measures and maximum spironolactone/furosemide doses.

Clinical reasoning

This meets the lecture's description of diuretic-resistant ascites. Review non-compliance, infection (SBP/TB) and malignancy; options listed include therapeutic paracentesis with/without albumin, TIPS and transplantation.
