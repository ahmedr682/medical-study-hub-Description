Ascites

Definition, causes, SAAG classification, pathophysiology, assessment, complications and treatment — based on the uploaded 26-page lecture.

Lecture module
Lecture notes · Mind map · Flashcards · MCQs · Clinical cases

1. Definition & causes

Ascites is abnormal accumulation of fluid in the abdomen; the lecture defines it as more than 25 mL in the peritoneal cavity.

Generalized causes: hepatic (cirrhosis/hepatic venous outflow obstruction), cardiac (congestive heart failure/constrictive pericarditis), renal (nephrotic syndrome), and nutritional hypoalbuminemia.

Local causes: malignancy, tuberculosis, pancreatic disease, bowel perforation and Meigs syndrome.

Differential of abdominal enlargement: obesity, pregnancy, ovarian cyst and intestinal obstruction.

2. Classification by SAAG

SAAG = serum albumin − ascitic fluid albumin.

  • High SAAG ≥1.1 g/dL: portal hypertensive ascites; hepatic and cardiac causes.
  • Low SAAG <1.1 g/dL: non-portal hypertensive ascites; renal, malignant and inflammatory causes.

Use paired serum and ascitic albumin measurements.

3. Pathophysiology

Portal hypertension raises splanchnic capillary hydrostatic pressure, promoting fluid movement into the peritoneal cavity. Effective circulating volume falls, renal perfusion decreases and compensatory systems are activated:

  • RAAS activation → increased aldosterone → sodium retention.
  • Sympathetic nervous system activation.
  • Increased tubular sodium reabsorption and water retention.

The lecture contrasts protein/LDH-rich inflammatory or malignant exudates with low-protein transudative fluid in portal hypertension.

4. Clinical features & examination

  • Symptoms: weight gain, increasing abdominal distension and dyspnea.
  • Look for cirrhosis stigmata: muscle wasting, palmar erythema and spider naevi.
  • Inspection: obtuse subcostal angle, divarication of recti, downward umbilical displacement, abdominal hernias, feminine hair distribution and dilated abdominal veins.
  • Percussion: shifting dullness or transmitted thrill; pleural effusion may coexist.
  • Auscultation: rare venous hum between xiphisternum and umbilicus due to paraumbilical vein recanalization.
  • Peripheral edema usually follows ascites in the lecture.

5. Investigations

  • Ultrasound: detects/quantifies ascites and evaluates liver, portal vein and spleen.
  • CT: useful for abdominal masses.
  • Diagnostic paracentesis: assess appearance and fluid studies; the lecture describes normal fluid as clear/pale yellow and cirrhotic fluid as clear/straw-colored.
  • Ascitic fluid analysis includes albumin for SAAG, protein, LDH, pH, glucose and cell count/cytology as clinically indicated.
  • Laparoscopy is shown as an additional diagnostic modality in the lecture.

6. Complications

  • Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP): ascitic neutrophils ≥250/mm³; abdominal pain and fever are common symptoms.
  • Hepatic hydrothorax, commonly right-sided.
  • Abdominal hernias, commonly umbilical.
  • Refractory ascites.
  • Hepatorenal syndrome (HRS).

7. Treatment principles in the lecture

  • High-SAAG cirrhotic ascites: salt restriction is the initial dietary measure.
  • Water restriction is considered in the lecture when serum sodium is below 130 mmol/L.
  • Spironolactone is described as the drug of choice because it blocks aldosterone receptors; furosemide is combined according to response and monitoring.
  • Tense/refractory ascites may require therapeutic paracentesis; IV albumin is generally administered in proportion to the amount removed.
  • Refractory ascites: consider TIPS in selected patients and liver transplantation; treat precipitating causes such as infection or malignancy.
  • Low-SAAG ascites generally does not respond to salt manipulation/diuretics; treat the underlying cause and use paracentesis when needed.

The lecture's doses are not a substitute for current protocols or individual clinical assessment.

Clinical Cases

Case 1 — New abdominal distension

A patient with cirrhosis develops progressive abdominal enlargement and dyspnea. Confirm fluid with ultrasound and perform diagnostic paracentesis; calculate SAAG to support classification.

Case 2 — Fever in a patient with ascites

A patient with cirrhotic ascites develops fever and abdominal discomfort. SBP must be considered; ascitic fluid neutrophils ≥250/mm³ meets the diagnostic threshold in the lecture.

Case 3 — Low SAAG

A patient has ascites with SAAG 0.7 g/dL. This is non-portal hypertensive by the lecture classification; consider malignant, renal or inflammatory causes and investigate the underlying condition.
