Nutrition in Chronic Liver Disease

Malnutrition, nutritional assessment, dietary requirements, micronutrients, hepatic encephalopathy and bone disease — based on the uploaded Assiut University lecture.

Lecture module
Lecture notes · Mind map · Flashcards · MCQs · Clinical cases

1. Definitions & clinical importance

  • Malnutrition/undernutrition: inadequate intake or uptake causing altered body composition, including reduced fat-free and body cell mass.
  • Muscle wasting: active progressive loss of muscle mass due to underlying disease, ultimately causing atrophy.
  • Sarcopenia: generalized reduction in muscle mass and function; may be primary (aging) or secondary (acute/chronic illness, including CLD).

Nutrition has a central prognostic and therapeutic role in liver disease. Malnutrition is common in cirrhosis and progresses alongside liver failure.

2. Burden of malnutrition

Severe malnutrition is associated with increased susceptibility to infection, hepatic encephalopathy and ascites. It is associated with lower survival in cirrhosis and HCC, and in patients undergoing liver transplantation; it also increases waiting-list mortality and post-transplant complications.

3. Screening & assessment

Advanced CLD, especially decompensated cirrhosis, should receive rapid nutritional screening. The lecture identifies two high-risk criteria:

  • BMI <18.5 kg/m².
  • Advanced decompensated cirrhosis (Child–Pugh C).

For high-risk patients, detailed assessment is suggested every 1–6 months:

  1. BMI: weight/height²; underweight <18.5, normal 18.5–24.9, overweight 25–29.9, obesity ≥30 kg/m². Limitations include abdominal obesity, short stature and unusually high muscle mass.
  2. Muscle mass/function: CT at L3, anthropometry (MAMC, MAMA, TSF), DEXA, bioelectrical impedance and handgrip strength.
  3. Subjective Global Assessment (SGA): clinical assessment including Royal Free Hospital-GA.
  4. Patient-reported intake: quality/quantity, supplements, fluids, sodium, meal timing and barriers to eating.

4. Nutritional management in cirrhosis

Cirrhosis is described as a state of accelerated starvation: protein synthesis decreases while gluconeogenesis from amino acids increases, promoting proteolysis and sarcopenia.

  • Protein: optimal daily intake 1.2–1.5 g/kg actual body weight/day.
  • Include late-evening oral nutritional supplementation and breakfast in malnourished patients with decompensated cirrhosis.
  • Split intake into 3 main meals plus 3 snacks (mid-morning, mid-afternoon and late evening; late evening is emphasized).
  • Include fruits and vegetables.
  • Restrict salt to approximately 80 mmol/day as stated in the lecture.
  • With HE, animal protein may be less well tolerated than vegetable and dairy protein.
  • Adjust diet for comorbidities such as diabetes.

5. Sarcopenia & micronutrients

Potential sarcopenia management listed: oral calorie/protein/amino-acid supplements, branched-chain amino acids (BCAA) and a structured exercise programme.

Vitamin deficiencies relate to hepatic dysfunction and reduced reserves. Vitamin A and D deficiencies are common in transplant candidates. Vitamin D <20 ng/mL is reported in chronic cholestatic conditions and severe cirrhosis.

Hyponatremia is common in cirrhosis; the lecture emphasizes careful monitoring of sodium and water intake.

6. Nutrition in hepatic encephalopathy

  • HE is more common in malnourished patients with cirrhosis.
  • Hyperammonemia may impair muscle function and contribute to muscle loss.
  • Avoid protein restriction in patients with HE.
  • Encourage vegetables and dairy protein.
  • Consider BCAA supplementation to improve neuropsychiatric performance.
  • In grade III–IV HE when oral intake is not possible, provide nutrition via nasogastric tube or parenterally.

7. Chronic liver disease & bone disease

Risk factors for osteoporosis listed include alcohol abuse, smoking, BMI <19 kg/m², male hypogonadism, early menopause, secondary amenorrhea >6 months, family history of osteoporotic fracture, prolonged corticosteroid use and advanced age.

WHO gold standard diagnosis: bone densitometry of lumbar spine and hip.

Management: balanced diet with calcium and vitamin D to preserve normal levels, physical activity (especially exercises improving spinal mechanics), and minimizing factors that increase bone loss.

8. Take-home messages

  • Healthy varied eating is advisable for all patients.
  • Except for alcohol, virtually no food damages the liver (as phrased in the lecture).
  • Adequate calories and protein are generally more important than avoiding specific foods.
  • Do not routinely reduce total protein intake in cirrhosis.
  • Lecture target: sodium about 80 mmol/day = 2 g sodium, corresponding to about 5 g added salt daily.
  • Skeletal muscle is the largest protein store; adequate intake may be difficult in advanced disease and sarcopenia.

Clinical Cases

Case 1 — Weight loss in decompensated cirrhosis

A patient with Child–Pugh C cirrhosis has reduced intake and low BMI. Screen promptly and assess muscle mass/function, dietary intake and SGA; the lecture suggests repeating detailed assessment every 1–6 months in high-risk patients.

Case 2 — Cirrhosis with hepatic encephalopathy

A patient with HE is eating poorly. Do not automatically restrict protein; encourage vegetable and dairy protein, consider BCAA, and use NG/parenteral nutrition if grade III–IV HE prevents oral intake.

Case 3 — Bone health

A patient with chronic liver disease has risk factors for osteoporosis. Assess bone density at lumbar spine and hip; support calcium/vitamin D adequacy and physical activity.
