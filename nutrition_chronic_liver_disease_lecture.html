Section 1

Definitions and burden of malnutrition in CLD

Introduction

Nutrition has a central prognostic and therapeutic role in management of patients with liver disease.

Terms and definitions

  • Malnutrition / undernutrition: a nutrition-related disorder resulting from lack of intake or uptake of nutrition, leading to altered body composition (decreased fat-free mass) and body cell mass.
  • Muscle wasting: active, progressive loss of muscle mass due to an underlying disease, ultimately leading to muscle atrophy.
  • Sarcopenia: generalized reduction in muscle mass and function due to aging (primary sarcopenia) or acute/chronic illness (secondary sarcopenia), including CLD.

Burden

  • Malnutrition is frequent in cirrhosis.
  • Progression of malnutrition is associated with progression of liver failure.
  • Severe malnutrition is associated with susceptibility to infections, hepatic encephalopathy and ascites.
  • It is associated with low survival in cirrhosis, HCC and patients undergoing liver transplantation; it increases waiting-list mortality and post-transplant complications.
Section 2

Screening and assessment for malnutrition

Patients with advanced CLD, especially decompensated cirrhosis, should undergo rapid nutritional screening.

Two high-risk criteria

  • Underweight: BMI <18.5 kg/m².
  • Advanced decompensated cirrhosis: Child–Pugh C.

In high-risk patients, detailed nutritional assessment of each component is recommended every 1–6 months.

1. Body mass index (BMI)

BMI = body mass divided by the square of body height (kg/m²).

CategoryBMI (kg/m²)
Underweight<18.5
Normal18.5–24.9
Overweight25–29.9
Obese≥30

Limitations: abdominal obesity, short stature and unusually high muscle mass.

2. Muscle mass and function

  • CT image analysis at lumbar vertebra L3 for direct skeletal muscle mass quantification.
  • Anthropometry: mid-arm muscle circumference (MAMC), mid-arm muscular area (MAMA), triceps skinfold (TSF).
  • Whole-body DEXA: bone mineral density, fat mass and fat-free mass.
  • Tetrapolar bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA): limb non-fat mass quantification.
  • Handgrip strength: skeletal muscle contractile function.

3. Subjective global assessment (SGA)

Uses clinical evaluation data to determine nutritional status; includes Royal Free Hospital–Global Assessment (RFH-GA).

4. Patient-reported dietary intake

Assess quality and quantity of food and supplements, fluids, sodium in the diet, number and timing of meals and barriers to eating.

Section 3

Nutritional management principles in liver cirrhosis

Why cirrhosis promotes muscle loss

The lecture describes cirrhosis as a state of accelerated starvation. Protein synthesis decreases and gluconeogenesis from amino acids increases, necessitating proteolysis; this contributes to sarcopenia and/or failure to respond to standard supplementation.

Energy and protein requirements

  • Optimal daily protein intake: 1.2–1.5 g/kg actual body weight/day.
  • Include late-evening oral nutritional supplementation and breakfast in the dietary regimen of malnourished patients with decompensated cirrhosis.
  • Split food intake into three main meals (breakfast, lunch, dinner) and three snacks (mid-morning, mid-afternoon, late evening); the late-evening snack is emphasized as most important.
  • Fruit and vegetables should always be included.
  • Salt restriction: approximately 80 mmol/day.
  • In hepatic encephalopathy, animal proteins may be less well tolerated than vegetable and dairy proteins.
  • Concomitant diseases such as diabetes mellitus may require dietary adjustments.

Potential management of sarcopenia

  • Oral supplements: calories, protein and amino acids.
  • Branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) supplements.
  • Structured exercise programme.
Section 4

Micronutrients in chronic liver disease

  • Vitamin deficiencies in liver disease are generally related to hepatic dysfunction and diminished reserves.
  • Most patients considered for liver transplantation present with vitamin A and D deficiencies.
  • Low vitamin D (<20 ng/mL) is reported in chronic cholestatic conditions and severe liver cirrhosis.
  • Vitamin D also correlates with treatment response in hepatitis C virus (HCV), as stated in the lecture.
  • Hyponatremia is common in cirrhosis, and is more likely when sodium intake is low while water intake is unchanged or increased. Careful monitoring of sodium and water intake is required.
Section 5

Nutritional treatment options for hepatic encephalopathy

  • HE is more common in malnourished patients with cirrhosis.
  • Hyperammonemia may impair muscle function and contribute to muscle loss.
  • Avoid protein restriction in patients with HE.
  • Encourage vegetables and dairy protein.
  • BCAA supplementation should be considered to improve neuropsychiatric performance.
  • In grade III–IV encephalopathy when patients are unable to eat, provide nutrition by nasogastric tube or parenterally.
Key exam point: The lecture explicitly says to avoid protein restriction in HE.
Section 6

Bone disease and take-home messages

Osteoporosis risk factors in CLD

  • Alcohol abuse and smoking.
  • BMI <19 kg/m².
  • Male hypogonadism.
  • Early menopause.
  • Secondary amenorrhea for more than 6 months.
  • Family history of osteoporotic fracture.
  • Prolonged corticosteroid treatment.
  • Advanced age.

Diagnosis

According to WHO, bone densitometry of the lumbar spine and hip is the gold standard for diagnosis of osteoporosis and osteopenia.

Management

  • Balanced diet including calcium and vitamin D supplements to preserve normal levels.
  • Physical activity, especially exercises improving spinal mechanics.
  • Minimize factors that increase bone loss.

Take-home messages from the lecture

  • Healthy eating with a variety of foods is advisable for all patients.
  • Except for alcohol, virtually no food damages the liver (lecture wording).
  • Adequate calories and protein are usually more important than avoiding specific foods.
  • Reducing total protein intake is not advisable in cirrhosis.
  • Recommended sodium intake: approximately 80 mmol/day = 2 g sodium, corresponding to 5 g added salt daily.
  • Skeletal muscle is the largest protein store in the body.
  • Adequate calorie and protein intake can be difficult in malnourished patients with sarcopenia and advanced liver disease.

Clinical Cases

Practice connecting lecture facts to a clinical presentation.

Case 1

Case — Malnutrition risk in decompensated cirrhosis

A patient with advanced decompensated cirrhosis has BMI 17.6 kg/m² and reduced food intake.

Clinical reasoning

Both BMI <18.5 and Child–Pugh C are high-risk criteria in the lecture. Perform rapid screening and detailed assessment including muscle mass/function, SGA and dietary intake; repeat every 1–6 months in high-risk patients.

Case 2

Case — Hepatic encephalopathy and poor intake

A patient with cirrhosis and HE is eating poorly. A plan is proposed to markedly restrict protein.

Clinical reasoning

This conflicts with the lecture, which says avoid protein restriction in HE. Encourage vegetables and dairy protein, consider BCAA, and in grade III–IV HE when unable to eat use NG or parenteral nutrition.

Case 3

Case — Bone health in chronic liver disease

A patient with CLD has risk factors for osteoporosis and asks how it is assessed.

Clinical reasoning

The lecture identifies bone densitometry of lumbar spine and hip as the gold standard. Management includes balanced diet with calcium/vitamin D, physical activity and minimizing bone-loss risk factors.
