Section 1

Definition, types and etiology

Learning objectives in the lecture

  1. Define portal hypertension and describe its etiology and types.
  2. Diagnose PH clinically and by investigations.
  3. Know its complications and management.

Definition

Clinical definition: Portal hypertension (PH) is pathological elevation of portal venous pressure resulting from obstruction to portal blood flow. The obstruction may be prehepatic, intrahepatic or posthepatic.

Laboratory definition: Hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) ≥6 mmHg. HVPG >10 mmHg is clinically significant; >12 mmHg is associated with complications.

TypeCauses listed in the lecture
PrehepaticPortal vein thrombosis; splenic vein thrombosis.
IntrahepaticLiver cirrhosis (stated as the most common cause in Egypt); hepatosplenic schistosomiasis; massive hepatic metastases; hepatic sinusoidal obstruction syndrome (SOS), previously called hepatic veno-occlusive disease.
PosthepaticBudd–Chiari syndrome; right-sided heart failure; constrictive pericarditis.
Clinical connection: The site of resistance helps organize the differential diagnosis: before the liver (prehepatic), within liver tissue/sinusoids (intrahepatic), or after hepatic venous drainage (posthepatic).
Section 2

Clinical features and portosystemic anastomoses

Depending on the cause, PH may be acute or chronic:

  • Acute PH: arises from acute portal vein thrombosis.
  • Chronic PH: may result from chronic thrombosis, cirrhosis or schistosomiasis.

Patients may also show symptoms and signs of the underlying disease, e.g. cirrhosis or right-sided heart failure.

Increased flow through portosystemic anastomoses

Venous pathwayClinical result
Paraumbilical and epigastric veinsCaput medusae and venous hum.
Rectal veinsHemorrhoidal / anorectal varices.
Gastric fundus and distal one-third of esophagusEsophageal and gastric varices. Esophageal varices may cause life-threatening bleeding (hematemesis); gastric varices are associated with melena in the lecture.

Other features

  • Congestive splenomegaly followed by hypersplenism, e.g. thrombocytopenia.
  • Upper GI bleeding from portal hypertensive gastropathy or GI ulcers, and diffuse lower GI bleeding.
  • Transudative ascites.
Section 3

Diagnosis of portal hypertension

Clinical manifestations of PH (e.g. ascites) in a patient with a known risk factor (e.g. cirrhosis) may already suffice for diagnosis. Investigations also assess the underlying condition.

1. Abdominal ultrasound / duplex

  • Specific finding: cavernous transformation of the portal vein on duplex indicates chronic portal vein thrombosis.
  • Unspecific finding: portal vein dilatation to >13 mm.
  • Indirect indications: increased flow through portosystemic anastomoses on duplex, splenomegaly and ascites.

2. Abdominal CT scan

  • Portal vein thrombosis is visible.
  • Searches for hepatic focal lesions suggestive of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).

3. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD)

  • Assesses esophageal or gastric varices and allows treatment if necessary.
  • Searches for congestive gastropathy and peptic ulcers.
Section 4

Treatment of portal hypertension

A. Medical treatment — non-selective beta blocker

The lecture gives propranolol as an example. It inhibits beta-2 adrenergic receptors in the gastrointestinal/splanchnic circulation, causing splanchnic vasoconstriction, reducing portal and collateral blood flow and therefore lowering portal hypertension. It is also used to prevent variceal bleeding.

The lecture describes titration to a dose that decreases the patient's baseline heart rate by 25%.

Important caution from the lecture: In advanced liver cirrhosis (Child–Pugh C), beta-blockers may cause circulatory dysregulation. If negative effects outweigh benefits, treatment should be reconsidered.

B. Portosystemic shunts

1. TIPS / TIPSS — transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt

Indications listed: persistent, recurrent or treatment-resistant upper GI bleeding due to PH (e.g. esophageal varices); refractory ascites; acute portal vein thrombosis; and selected patients with hepatorenal syndrome who are awaiting or not eligible for liver transplantation.

Contraindications listed: pre-existing hepatic encephalopathy or Child–Pugh C cirrhosis (reduced hepatic ammonia elimination may worsen encephalopathy); heart failure; severe pulmonary hypertension >45 mmHg; uncontrolled systemic infection/sepsis; hepatic cysts or tumors; cavernous transformation of the portal vein following thrombosis.

2. Total portosystemic shunt: portal vein is completely shunted to the vena cava, reducing portal pressure.

3. Selective portosystemic shunt: portal vein is partially shunted to the vena cava, reducing portal pressure; the partial shunt aims to prevent varices while allowing portal perfusion to continue.

Section 5

Complications of portal hypertension

  1. Esophageal variceal hemorrhage: bleeding from dilated submucosal veins of the distal esophagus; a dangerous consequence of PH and the most common form of upper GI bleeding in patients presenting with liver cirrhosis, according to the lecture.
  2. Portal hypertensive gastropathy: associated with cirrhosis/PH or portal vein thrombosis; may cause GI ulcers or diffuse lower GI bleeding as described in the lecture.
  3. Ascites.
  4. Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis.
  5. Hepatorenal syndrome.
  6. Gastric antral vascular ectasia.
  7. Pulmonary complications of PH.
  8. Cardiac cirrhosis.

Clinical Cases

Practice connecting lecture facts to a clinical presentation.

Case 1

Case — Cirrhosis with hematemesis

A patient with known cirrhosis presents with upper GI bleeding and splenomegaly.

Clinical reasoning

Think of portal hypertension with possible esophageal variceal hemorrhage. The lecture supports EGD to assess varices and allow treatment; also consider other PH manifestations such as hypersplenism and ascites.

Case 2

Case — Dilated abdominal wall veins

A patient has prominent veins radiating around the umbilicus and a venous hum.

Clinical reasoning

This matches increased flow through paraumbilical and epigastric portosystemic anastomoses, producing caput medusae and sometimes a venous hum.

Case 3

Case — Portal vein duplex finding

Duplex ultrasound shows cavernous transformation of the portal vein.

Clinical reasoning

The lecture identifies cavernous transformation as a specific finding indicating chronic portal vein thrombosis. CT can also visualize PVT.
