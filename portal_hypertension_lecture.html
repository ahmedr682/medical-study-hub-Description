Portal Hypertension

Definition, causes, clinical manifestations, diagnosis, complications and management — based on the 36-page lecture by Prof. Saad Zaky Mahmoud.

Lecture module
Lecture notes · Mind map · Flashcards · MCQs · Clinical cases

1. Definition & hemodynamic thresholds

Portal hypertension (PH) is pathological elevation of portal venous pressure due to obstruction of portal blood flow. It may be prehepatic, intrahepatic or posthepatic.

  • HVPG ≥ 6 mmHg: PH definition in this lecture.
  • HVPG > 10 mmHg: clinically significant PH.
  • HVPG > 12 mmHg: associated with complications.

Think of the portal system as blood arriving at the liver; resistance anywhere along the route raises upstream pressure.

2. Etiology by site

Prehepatic
  • Portal vein thrombosis
  • Splenic vein thrombosis
Intrahepatic
  • Liver cirrhosis — listed as the most common cause in Egypt
  • Hepatosplenic schistosomiasis
  • Massive hepatic metastases
  • Sinusoidal obstruction syndrome (SOS)
Posthepatic
  • Budd–Chiari syndrome
  • Right-sided heart failure
  • Constrictive pericarditis

3. Clinical features & portosystemic anastomoses

  • May be acute (e.g. acute portal vein thrombosis) or chronic (chronic thrombosis, cirrhosis, schistosomiasis).
  • Underlying disease manifestations.
  • Paraumbilical ↔ epigastric veins: caput medusae and venous hum.
  • Rectal veins: anorectal varices.
  • Gastric fundus and distal one-third esophagus: gastric/esophageal varices; esophageal varices may cause life-threatening hematemesis; gastric varices are associated with melena in the lecture.
  • Congestive splenomegaly → hypersplenism, e.g. thrombocytopenia.
  • Portal hypertensive gastropathy, GI ulceration/bleeding and transudative ascites.

4. Diagnosis

  • Clinical PH manifestations in a patient with a risk factor such as cirrhosis may be sufficient to suspect/diagnose PH.
  • Abdominal duplex ultrasound: cavernous transformation suggests chronic portal vein thrombosis; portal vein diameter >13 mm is a nonspecific finding; collateral flow, splenomegaly and ascites are indirect signs.
  • CT abdomen: demonstrates portal vein thrombosis and evaluates focal liver lesions suspicious for HCC.
  • EGD: assesses esophageal/gastric varices and portal hypertensive gastropathy; may allow endoscopic treatment.

5. Management in the lecture

  • Non-selective beta blocker (propranolol): beta-2 blockade causes splanchnic vasoconstriction, reducing portal/collateral flow; also used for prevention of variceal bleeding. The lecture describes titration to a 25% reduction in baseline heart rate.
  • In advanced Child–Pugh C cirrhosis, beta blockers may worsen circulatory dysregulation; reassess risk versus benefit.
  • TIPS: considered for persistent/recurrent/treatment-resistant upper GI bleeding, refractory ascites, acute portal vein thrombosis and selected HRS patients awaiting/not eligible for transplant.
  • Lecture-listed TIPS contraindications: pre-existing encephalopathy/Child C, heart failure, severe pulmonary hypertension (>45 mmHg), uncontrolled infection/sepsis, hepatic cysts/tumors and cavernous transformation after portal vein thrombosis.
  • Total and selective portosystemic shunts are also described.

This is a study summary of the lecture, not a patient-specific prescribing protocol.

6. Complications

  1. Esophageal variceal hemorrhage — major upper GI bleeding complication.
  2. Portal hypertensive gastropathy.
  3. Ascites.
  4. Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis.
  5. Hepatorenal syndrome.
  6. Gastric antral vascular ectasia.
  7. Pulmonary complications of PH.
  8. Cardiac cirrhosis (as listed in the lecture).

Clinical Cases

Case 1 — Upper GI bleeding

A patient with cirrhosis presents with hematemesis. Think of esophageal variceal hemorrhage as a major PH complication. The lecture emphasizes EGD for assessment and treatment, alongside PH-directed management.

Case 2 — Splenomegaly and low platelets

A patient has splenomegaly and thrombocytopenia with chronic liver disease. Connect congestive splenomegaly to hypersplenism in chronic PH.

Case 3 — Dilated abdominal wall veins

Prominent veins radiate from the umbilicus. Identify caput medusae due to paraumbilical–epigastric collateral flow.
